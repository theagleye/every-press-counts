Every press counts

Europress compactors already count every stroke of the ram. Each stroke pushes one charge of waste. Strokes times kilos per stroke gives the weight. Each site is taught once with 3 weighed lifts. No permanent scale, no daily operator data. All data here is Europress's synthetic dataset and simulator, with its hypothetical prices.

1

Only the operator knows the weight

The customer pays a fixed fee per lift. The weight is measured only at the operator's weighbridge, after the truck has left. The customer sees it late, or not at all.

2

The fill reading lifts too early

SMART orders a lift when the fill reading reaches its threshold. In Europress's model the reading is off on every machine:

  • each machine has its own gain error (spread 7%) and offset (spread 4 points);
  • plastics spring back and read 4–5 points high;
  • the reading wobbles, and the first wobble past the threshold triggers the lift.

Lifting by kilos waits until the container really holds 80% of what it can take.

3

Lifting by kilos pays: one real machine, 56 days

One decision case from Europress's simulator: a 10 m³ DUO compactor collecting plastic film. The simulator ran the next 56 days six times for each policy and averaged the results. Across all cases, weight adds 23% to the value of the best decision without weight.

4

The compactor already weighs itself

Every stroke of the ram pushes one charge of waste into the container. The controller starts every stroke, so it can count them. Strokes times the average kilos per stroke for that waste stream gives the weight.

A cook who knows one spoon of sugar is about 10 g knows what is in the bowl by counting spoons. The spoon is one stroke. The 10 g is kilos per stroke. Counting is the stroke counter SMART already logs.

7

What it is worth, and how it breaks

I rebuilt Europress's world with its own public generator, changing one thing at a time. Value is what lifting by kilos adds on top of the best decision without weight, per machine per year.

8

What if every site presses differently?

Some people fill the hopper before they press. Others press after one banana box. I broke the real stroke counts on purpose: every site got its own random habit. Then I tested ways to get the weight back. 20% of machines teach, 80% are scored against their weighbridge tickets.

9

The design rules

  1. Read slightly high. The count is scaled by 1.1. Reading high lifts a little early. Reading low overflows.
  2. Catch gaps. Missing telemetry means missing strokes. Below 95% coverage the estimate is marked low confidence and SMART falls back to the fill reading at 90%, Europress's own fallback.
  3. Check capacity. An estimate above 1.3 × the container's capacity in kilos is an over-read, usually strokes from more than one fill. It is marked low confidence.
  4. Watch the fill sensor. Fill at 85% or more while kilos are under 40% means the fill sensor is stuck. SMART ignores it and opens a service ticket.
  5. Kilos decide, fill guards. Lift at 80% of capacity in kilos. The fill reading only forces a lift at 98%, to stop an overflow.
  6. Teach each site once. 3 weighed lifts set the site's kilos per stroke: 3 tickets asked for once, or a mobile calibration kit. A stream average from a few reference machines is the start value.
  7. Re-weigh on drift. When strokes and fill disagree by more than 2× for 3 lifts in a row, SMART asks for one new weighing and uses fill meanwhile. Stable sites need about one re-weigh every 11 to 14 years.
  8. Two channels check each other. Strokes give mass, the fill reading or ram travel gives volume. A sudden change in density flags rain, a wrong stream or drift.
  9. Every number says what it is. Each record states measured or estimated, the method and the confidence, in the FIWARE Smart Data Models format.
10

The rules on a year of SMART data

I replayed the rules hour by hour on the dataset's telemetry for all 447 machines. Each hour uses only the readings up to that hour, so the same code can run live in the SMART cloud. One cardboard machine shows all the rules at work.

11

Where it pays

  • Every connected machine: the stroke estimate as a SMART software update. No permanent hardware. Each site taught once: 3 tickets, or one kit visit.
  • A few reference machines: a real gauge, fitted at the factory or during a planned service, to teach kilos per stroke.
  • No gauge on every machine: in the simulator, a stroke count taught per site keeps 94% of a gauge's value even when sites press 1× to 20× as often. A permanent gauge adds the last 6%.
  • Beyond lifts: machines differ 12.4× in strokes per year, so service can follow use instead of the calendar. 88 machines are usually lifted half empty and 46 past full: leads for a different size or on-call lifting. Stuck sensors, drift and pressing habits become service tickets and customer coaching.
  • Rollout: a one-day desk check on Europress's real cycle counts, a pilot on 20 to 30 plastics and mixed waste machines, then a software launch stream by stream.
12

The whole loop

The weighbridge keeps the bill, where the law needs an approved scale. The compactor keeps its own count: good enough to decide when to lift, free to run on every machine, and the customer's own record of what left the site.

What this rests on, and what it doesn't

  • All data is synthetic, and all euro figures use the dataset's hypothetical prices.
  • The simulator creates strokes from deposits with a fixed mean kilos per deposit for each stream, so stroke counting works there partly by construction. Real charges vary more.
  • Real SMART logging of cycle counts is assumed, not confirmed. If missing, it is a controller change.
  • The x1.1 setting was tuned on the simulator and needs real data.
  • The pressing habits in step 8 are injected by me. The dataset gives every site the same habit. Only a pilot shows real habits.
  • The stroke count does not meet the brief's ±100 kg on 3 t target. It is good enough to decide when to lift, not to check an operator's tonnage. That needs the reference gauges.
  • The watchdog thresholds were picked by hand on this dataset.
  • Weighing each stroke from its pressure curve is the next step and is untested: the dataset has no pressure curves.
